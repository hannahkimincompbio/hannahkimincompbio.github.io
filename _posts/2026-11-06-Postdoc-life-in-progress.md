My postdoc life has started approximately one month ago. Some say that to be a Ph.D. degree holder--hence "post"-doc--is to finally prove one's independence, but I cannot but still feel like a pullet; not fully an adult, not really a chick either. Would I ever feel like a fully grown ~chicken~ researcher one day? I have tried doing a lot of different things to see how independent I am lately. 

If I write a solo-author paper on your own, am I independent? Today was an interesting day, because I received one rejection and one acceptance. One rejection was from a top conference for a full paper that I wrote alone over the summer. As it should, it gave me a lot of information about the conference atmosphere; what I mean is that it was obvious that the reviewers were real people, not AI, and I am fortunate to get their real-world perspectives. As a researcher in the era of GenAI reviews, this was important. Surely, it would have been nice if the paper were viewed more favorably, but I guess I am only as luck as I could be. I do see a glimmer of hope in the past two rejections that I got from that conference, though, because this is the second time that I convinced at least one out of three complete strangers that my work was interesting. I must be close to getting it right. I can still try for a poster too, and I feel like I could be at an advantage for that because I got those feedbacks. On the other hand, one acceptance was from another conference for an abstract, which was a resubmission of a prior work that did not get published. Many people worked hard on that paper (including me grinding over Christmas and the New Year's day), and there was something still alive about that paper--despite the fast pace of AI development in the past year--that had to be announced loudly to the world. Now I get to improve my argument with a larger page limit. Both papers were to have deadlines close to one another in December, so it could be that things have naturally figured things out, making things manageable. At the same time, it is good to know that these two conferences with similar deadlines exist.

If I suddenly realize that having access to GPUs is to be able to run local large-language models and try all things that is agentic, am I independent? To be honest, the information about existence of GPU has not rung my bell for like a month. The supercomputing research center webpage clearly states that they have 6 nodes with dual GPUs, and the research center people informed users on their 'Using LLMs for researchers' seminar that AI can be used on the cluster with luck. I had prior experience using Ollama with remote servers provided by AI companies after realizing that running even the smallest local LLM on my Macbook M1 Pro was not feasible with the patience of the 2026 computer users. Heck, I even felt a strong sense of fomo over the summer when I could not use a computer vision model without a GPU. When a friend of mine recently gave a seminar about an AI coding agent called Hermes which many people raved about on Threads, I got amused but felt disconnected because I did not have a computer with files that can be easily recovered if the agent were to mess things up or was I immediately bursting with ideas. That is why when I was given a project that eventually involved extensive debugging, I was taking mostly a traditional approach of debugging albeit with occasional help from in-browser ChatGPT. Then one evening, after spending the entire day bedridden after getting three vaccines at once, I finally came to a realization: having access to GPUs means that I can run LLMs locally! I spent the first day setting up Ollama on my HPC environment and using one of the best open-source models out there. Then I spent the next day enthusiastically configuring Hermes. I was able to easily debug the annoying bits of codes using Hermes. Now I can finally say that I have used harness engineering. There are loop engineering and graph engineering to be tried, but I think I can already benefit a lot from this level of complexity. It is not just me---my supervisor (out of the blue) asked me about the biomedical agent that I have been interested in, and asked me to dig literature on it. I probably still need to dig into the literature and read about biomedical agents that are out there, but it took me just one feverish night to realize that the Ollama local LLM setup with supercomputing center GPUs enables us to use that particular biomedical agent without additional spending. I have documented the installation step and even drew a figure to explain what I was doing. I have finally caught up, but I still have a problem. How do I let the world know that I know how to do things with AI agents? One way would be to communicate about AI knowledge on social media, but the obvious way is to build things that I have only dreamt of and announce the progress. What should I build? 

If I become aware of how funding works, am I independent? I would not call myself a complete novice to how funding works in academia, but my current status makes me limited in pursuit. Still, the pinnacle of independence that a postdoc can show is to get research funding, so I thought I needed to be aware of the foundations while I wait for the right opportunity. The university mailing list has been sending emails about a platform that one can find funding. I have gone to as many grant-writing seminars as I could afford to back when I was a doctoral student; something different had to happen on my end for me to understand the nature of grants. Recently, I impulsively visited a local library in-person in like four years of living in the same location. There is a lot of background to why I suddenly began aware of the library, but let's just say that the destiny has somehow connected me back to physical media in recent days. The library had a relatively new section in the back for local entrepreneurs. Amused by the existence of a rare third place, I began to check out book shelves and there I found a somewhat thick book about grant writing. The grant writing book was not completely focused on the research grants, but it provided interesting perspectives, so I used the library card that I happened to have to borrow it for three weeks. What I learned from that book so far sort of made it easy for me to deal with the rejection I got today. At the edge of implementing things in reality, there are a lot of human elements that need to be thought about; trust in personality and capability needs to be earned and details should be taken care of seriously. In that sense, doing good research and making a name during postdoc years would be necessary in persuading grant givers to have a basic level of trust in the researcher. To be independent potentially means to be trustworthy even without the supervisor name, although supervisor help would definitely add a lot. I don't know if this was the lesson that the authors intended, but I feel like I have a better understanding of communities in general because I began reading the book.

There are a lot of ifs and it is unclear what I would have in my hands by the end of this postdoc journey, but one can only try and find out.
