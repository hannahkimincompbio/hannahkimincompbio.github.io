It is the Thanksgiving week, and I am currently working on the paper for which the abstract was accepted. I had some quality time with family and friends, and that is something that I am grateful for this year. I would typically spend another day or two shopping the Black Friday discounts, but I am not quite in the mindset for it this year. I sometimes wonder if I had shopped the sales so passionately back in the days that I know too well what it is like to be shopping. Some deals that I am still eyeing are subscription-based; while enticing, I am not even taking the full advantage of the subscriptions that I already have, so I feel hesitant to add more. 

As I have written previously, the past several weeks were spent on using large-language models (LLMs) in bioinformatics research. The process gave me mixed signals about what experts can achieve with AI. The dominant message around using swarms of AI agents working in parallel is that the role of human is managing those agents. And yet, in the perspective of quality control, being able to identify nooks and crooks that matter does not feel like a typical managerial task. There is something both macro and micro about what constitutes a good AI use that it is honestly a bit confusing. Perhaps it is the macro and micro aspects of a project that are tricky to standardize in the first place. They are the factors that make difference, and they are the solutions to changes in the environment. Can one person be trained for that? All debates on AI taking jobs revolve around how not as many people are needed in the presence of AI. Would that free those few people from being a cog in the system, and be irreplaceable? Being irreplaceable is a leverage for individuals but a risk for the system that is dependent on them (e.g., multiple copies of tumor suppressor genes in elephants make them difficult to get cancer). Also, given the ability to easily achieve "standard" quality, is the landscape of our intellectual, artistic, and societal demands changing?

In the past week, I have realized a physical bottleneck for local LLMs: even with a GPU, most people could currently afford local models at around size of 30Gb. Gemma-4-31B is currently in this range, and so is Inkling-2-Mini released this week. Gemma-4-31B has been addressing my needs well, but it was not behaving as agentically (e.g., planning out workflows and knowing when to use specific tools) as GLM-5.3-Flash. Assuming that it was the matter of having an older model vs a newer model, I attempted to download GLM-5.3-Flash via vllm upon learning that its weights are available on Hugging Face. Only then did I realize that GLM-5.3-Flash has 320B parameters and is around 320Gb in size. Maybe we would look at 320Gb some day and find it small, but today wasn't the day. 

Given this situation, I decided not to reinvent the wheel for the literature review and gladly be a user of already well-thought-of designs. The two tools that I have been looking are Biomni [1] and Paperclip GXL [2]. It was much easier to look for datasets using Paperclip than to go through multiple databases myself. Using retrieval augmented generation (RAG), it took my query to gather information on data accessibility and generated a summary table. While it did not exactly return information that I was looking for, it showed how the majority of datasets were coming from cell lines; the research around the topic must be in its early days. It even returned information about a preprint published a couple of days ago, so it must have been kept up-to-date. I think I will be able to make a good use of it.
